Mini Aussie vs Standard Australian Shepherd: How Big Each One Really Gets

Heights and weights from the AKC breed standards, what “mini” and “toy” actually mean, and a quick height check.

Home›Blog›Mini Aussie vs Standard Size

Published: 2026-05-27 · Australian Shepherd Club

Quick answer: by the AKC breed standards, a Mini Aussie (registered by the AKC as the Miniature American Shepherd) stands 13–18 inches at the shoulder and weighs 20–40 pounds. A Standard Australian Shepherd stands 18–23 inches and weighs 40–65 pounds. The two ranges meet at 18 inches, and the sex of the dog decides which standard an 18–20-inch dog falls under.

Where does your dog's height fall?

Measure from the floor to the top of the shoulder blades (the withers) with the dog standing square, then compare it with both AKC standards. Puppies under about a year are still growing, so treat their result as provisional.

What do the breed standards actually say?

The figures below come straight from the AKC breed pages for the Australian Shepherd and the Miniature American Shepherd. Heights are measured at the withers. Weights are the AKC's typical ranges, not show requirements.

AKC height and weight ranges
Miniature American Shepherd (“Mini Aussie”)Australian Shepherd (standard)
Males, height14–18 in (36–46 cm)20–23 in (51–58 cm)
Females, height13–17 in (33–43 cm)18–21 in (46–53 cm)
Weight20–40 lb (9–18 kg)50–65 lb males, 40–55 lb females (23–29 / 18–25 kg)

Two details matter more than the headline ranges. First, the standards are split by sex, so a single “Mini Aussie is 13–18 inches” figure hides the fact that females top out at 17. Second, there is a gap for males: a male between 18 and 20 inches fits neither standard. Those dogs exist, and they are often sold as “mini” or “medium”.

Why “Mini Aussie” means different things to different breeders

The miniature began with small Australian Shepherds that were working in the United States in the 1960s and were bred down for size. The AKC page notes that the breed was originally called the Miniature Australian Shepherd. When the AKC took it in, the breed was renamed the Miniature American Shepherd, with its parent club chosen in 2011 and full recognition in July 2015.

Not everyone followed. As the Wikipedia history of the breed sums up, the Miniature Australian Shepherd community is still divided over that compromise, and at least one older Mini Aussie club no longer accepts dogs registered as Miniature American Shepherds with the AKC. In practice, a “Mini Aussie” puppy can come from an AKC Miniature American Shepherd line or from a separate registry with its own size rules. “Toy Aussie” has no AKC standard at all.

What about dogs between the two standards?

Look at the 18-inch line in the table. A female Australian Shepherd can be 18 inches; a male Miniature American Shepherd can be 18 inches too. So two dogs of the same height can each be correct for their own breed. Above that, a 19-inch male is too tall for the miniature standard and too short for the standard Aussie.

That matters when you buy. If a breeder advertises “minis” that mature at 19 or 20 inches, the dogs are not wrong as pets, but the label is doing marketing work, not describing a breed standard. Ask which standard the parents were bred to and what they actually measure.

How do you measure an Aussie's height correctly?

  1. Stand the dog on a hard, level floor with all four feet square under the body.
  2. Find the withers, the highest point of the shoulder blades, just behind the neck.
  3. Lay a straight edge or level flat across the withers and measure from the floor straight up to it.
  4. Measure two or three times and use the most consistent reading; a fluffy coat makes eyeballing unreliable.

Weight is a poorer guide than height because coat, condition and age all shift it. Use height to decide which standard a dog resembles, and weight to track body condition with your vet.

What changes day to day with the smaller size?

Size changes the logistics more than the dog. A Miniature American Shepherd needs a smaller crate and car space, eats less, and is easier to lift onto a vet table or into a car. Weight-based products such as parasite treatments come in smaller (and often cheaper) bands.

What size does not change is the herding brain. Both breeds were bred to work livestock, and owners of both describe the same need for daily exercise, training and a job to do. If you live in a flat, our guide to managing herding instinct in an apartment applies to minis as much as to standards, and our exercise needs by age breakdown shows how activity should change from puppy to adult.

Coat care is also the same: both have a double coat, so our answer on whether to shave an Australian Shepherd holds for either size.

Does a smaller Aussie mean less grooming?

Only in square footage. Both breeds carry a double coat with a weather-resistant outer layer and a soft undercoat, and both shed. The AKC's care notes for the Miniature American Shepherd say the breed sheds a fair amount, more during shedding season, which can come once or twice a year, and that mats can be worked out with a slicker brush or metal comb. For the standard Australian Shepherd, the AKC suggests an undercoat rake every two or three days during the heavy shed, followed by a wire brush, plus regular ear checks and tooth brushing.

So the routine is the same; a mini simply has less dog to brush. If you are choosing a mini because you hope for less hair on the sofa, expect a smaller pile, not a different kind of coat. The same applies to summer: the reasons not to shave a double coat do not depend on the dog's height.

Does a mini need less mental work?

No, and this is where buyers are most often surprised. The AKC's breed page for the Miniature American Shepherd explains that purpose-bred dogs have jobs that need decision-making and concentration, and that without that brain exercise they will create their own projects to keep busy. That description fits the standard Aussie just as well; both were selected from the same working stock.

In practice, a short training session, a puzzle feeder or a sniffing game counts as much as a long walk. Plan for both every day, whichever size you bring home. A smaller dog is easier to take along to more places, which can make that daily variety easier to provide, but it does not shrink the need for it.

Which questions should you ask a breeder about size?

  • Which registry and which standard are the parents bred to (AKC Australian Shepherd, AKC Miniature American Shepherd, or another club)?
  • What do the mother and father measure at the withers as adults, not just what they weigh?
  • How tall did previous puppies from the same pairing end up?
  • If the puppies are sold as “toy”, what size range does that mean, and which registry records it?

Common mistakes when comparing Mini and Standard Aussies

  • Treating “mini” as one size. AKC Miniature American Shepherds have a defined range; dogs sold as minis outside that registry may not.
  • Ignoring sex. The female and male ranges differ by an inch or more in both breeds.
  • Choosing by weight alone. Coat and condition make weight misleading; height at the withers is what the standards use.
  • Assuming a smaller dog is a calmer dog. Both are working herding breeds; plan the same daily exercise and training. If being left alone is a concern, see our Australian Shepherd separation anxiety article.

Frequently asked questions

How big is a Mini Aussie compared with a Standard Australian Shepherd?

Under the AKC standards, a Miniature American Shepherd (the AKC name for the Mini Aussie) stands 14–18 inches at the shoulder for males and 13–17 inches for females, and weighs 20–40 pounds. A Standard Australian Shepherd stands 20–23 inches for males and 18–21 inches for females, with 50–65 pounds for males and 40–55 pounds for females.

Is a Mini Aussie the same as a Miniature American Shepherd?

Usually, but not always. The AKC breed was originally called the Miniature Australian Shepherd and was renamed Miniature American Shepherd before full AKC recognition in 2015. Some breeders still register “Mini Aussies” with separate clubs that did not join the AKC breed, so ask which registry a puppy comes from.

Is a Toy Australian Shepherd a real breed?

Not in the AKC. Neither the Australian Shepherd nor the Miniature American Shepherd standard has a toy size, so “toy Aussie” describes a dog bred smaller than the Miniature American Shepherd standard, registered, if at all, outside the AKC.

My dog is 19 inches tall. Is it a mini or a standard?

Neither standard covers it exactly. A 19-inch male is above the Miniature American Shepherd range (14–18 in) and below the Australian Shepherd male range (20–23 in). A 19-inch female is inside the Australian Shepherd female range (18–21 in). The checker above shows where any height falls.

Do Mini Aussies need less exercise than Standard Aussies?

Both are herding breeds bred to work, so a smaller frame does not mean a low-energy dog. Size changes the scale of activity (stride, carrying weight, how far a walk tires them), not the need for daily exercise and mental work.

How can I estimate a puppy's adult size?

Ask the breeder for the measured adult heights of both parents and which standard they were bred to. A breed label alone is a weak predictor, especially for dogs sold as mini or toy outside a breed standard.

The bottom line

Mini Aussie vs Standard Australian Shepherd size comes down to two AKC standards: 13–18 inches and 20–40 pounds for the Miniature American Shepherd, 18–23 inches and 40–65 pounds for the Australian Shepherd. Check the sex-specific range, ask what the parents measure, and plan for the same working-dog energy whichever size you pick.
