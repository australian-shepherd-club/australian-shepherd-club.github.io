How Australian Shepherd Owners Set Up the Car: Crate vs Harness vs Back-Seat Hammock Compared

Most of us started with a loose dog on the back seat and a lot of hope. Here is how Aussie owners actually set up their cars, what each option does well, and how to switch to a better setup without a fight.

Every Australian Shepherd car setup comes down to three tools: a travel crate in the cargo area, a car harness clipped to a seat belt, or a back-seat hammock that turns the rear bench into a padded floor. If your Aussie currently paces the back seat, pokes its nose between the front seats or rides with its chin on your shoulder, you are in good company, and it is not too late to change the routine. This guide compares the three options honestly, including the parts nobody likes to admit, and gives you a planner that sizes a crate and ranks the setups for your dog and your car.

Quick answer: A fitted travel crate secured in the cargo area of an SUV, wagon or minivan is the most contained option for a medium-sized herding dog. A car harness on a short seat-belt tether suits sedans and dogs that settle well. A hammock is a comfort and cleanliness layer, not a restraint, so use it together with a harness, never instead of one. Whichever you choose, teach it at home and in a parked car first, then start with short drives.

Car setup planner: which option fits your Aussie and your car?

Enter two quick measurements and a few honest answers. The planner ranks crate, harness and hammock combinations, estimates a minimum crate size and checks it against your cargo space.

Treat the result as a starting point for a conversation with your own car and your own dog, not a final verdict. A dog that climbs into the front seat today is not a bad traveller; it is a dog nobody has shown where to ride yet.

Dog trainer Sarah, of the Sarah Certified channel, walks through the car she uses every week for her own dogs and client dogs, including the crate in the back, how she keeps it cool and how she organizes harnesses and leashes, which makes it easy to picture your own setup before you buy anything.

Why do so many Aussie owners start with a loose dog?

Because it is the easiest thing to do, and almost everyone does it at first. Survey data shows that restraint is the exception rather than the rule among dog owners who drive with their dogs.

In a national survey of 1,000 dog owners who had driven with their dog in the previous 12 months, AAA and Kurgo found that only 17 percent used any form of pet restraint. In the same survey, 31 percent admitted being distracted by their dog while driving, 59 percent had done at least one distracting thing such as petting the dog (55 percent), and 21 percent had let their dog sit in their lap. The release also cites AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety research that looking away from the road for just two seconds doubles the risk of a crash.

Admitting it is the first step

Those numbers are from 2010, but the habits they describe will sound familiar to anyone who has driven with an Aussie. Herding dogs want to see what is happening, and the front of the car is where the action is. If you have reached back to push a nose away at a red light, you are not a careless owner. You are an owner whose dog has never been given a better job in the car. The good news is that the fix is mostly training, not money.

Why Aussies make it harder

Aussies are built to watch moving things. Cyclists, joggers and other dogs flashing past the window can turn a calm dog into a spinning one in seconds. A setup that limits the view, gives a clear place to lie down and keeps the dog from reaching you is not a punishment; for many herding dogs it is the first time the car feels predictable. If your dog also struggles to settle at home, our guide to giving the herding drive a job indoors builds the same off switch you will rely on in the car.

Crate vs harness vs hammock: how do they compare?

A secured crate contains the dog completely, a car harness keeps it in one seat position, and a hammock only protects the seat and gives a stable surface. The right answer depends on your vehicle as much as on your dog.

Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center advises against letting a dog roam freely in the car and says the safest way to travel is in a crash-tested harness, seat belt or travel crate, pointing owners to the Center for Pet Safety for tested products. The travel crate: when is it the right choice?

A crate is the right choice when you have cargo space, your Aussie is busy or destructive in the car, or more than one dog rides with you. It is the setup most trainers and sport handlers end up with, because it turns the car into a familiar den.

Size it to the dog, not the biggest box that fits

The Australian Shepherd breed standard published by the FCI gives a preferred height at the withers of 20 to 23 inches for males and 18 to 21 inches for females, and many pet Aussies fall outside those ranges. That spread is why you should measure your own dog rather than buy by breed. A useful baseline is the one Cornell gives for airline crates, and it works in the car too: the dog should be able to stand, turn around and lie down comfortably. Our planner adds a few inches to your dog's sitting head height and body length as a rough guide; it is a planning rule of thumb, not a crash-test specification, so always check the maker's own sizing chart. If you are not sure whether your dog is a standard or a Mini, our comparison of Mini Aussie and standard Australian Shepherd sizes explains the overlap.

Anchor it, every time

A crate that slides is only half a setup. Use the cargo tie-down points and the straps designed for the crate, and push it up against the back of the rear seat. A 2015 CBS Philadelphia report on Center for Pet Safety crash tests noted that there are no federal testing or performance standards for pet travel crates, and that footage from the tests showed anchor straps breaking free and a hard-sided crate coming apart. In other words, "crash tested" on a box is a claim to check, not a guarantee; look for independent certification and follow the attachment method exactly as tested.

Teach the crate at home first

A dog that already loves its crate in the living room accepts the car crate much faster. Feed meals in it, toss chews in it and close the door for short, pleasant periods. Then move to the parked car with the engine off. Aussies that have struggled with being left alone sometimes find confinement hard, so give those dogs an even slower ramp, and never leave any dog crated in a parked car.

The car harness: when is it the right choice?

A car harness is the right choice for sedans, smaller cars and Aussies that lie down and stay down once the car moves. It keeps the dog close, which many owners like, but it only works if the fit and the tether length are right.

A walking harness is not a car harness

Your everyday walking harness is designed for pulling on a leash, not for holding a dog in a seat. Car harnesses have broader chest plates, stronger hardware and a dedicated attachment to the seat belt or a seat anchor. Cornell's list of safe options names crash-tested harnesses specifically, which is the standard to look for. If you already own a walking harness your dog loves, keep it for walks; our Front Range Flex review covers walking harness fit on an Aussie chest, but that is a separate job from riding in the car.

Keep the tether short

The tether should let your Aussie sit up and lie down, but not reach the front seats, the window gap or the floor in front of the seat. Attach to the back of the harness, never to a collar. Check the fit before each trip: two fingers under the straps, no slipping over the shoulders when you pull gently backwards.

Sit them behind the passenger seat

A common choice is to clip the dog behind the front passenger seat so they can see it in the mirror without turning around, and so the dog is not loaded from the traffic side of the street. Cornell also warns against letting a dog ride with its head out of the window, because of eye injuries from debris and the risk of being thrown during a sudden stop. A short tether solves that too.

Is a back-seat hammock enough on its own?

No. A hammock protects your upholstery, blocks the footwell gap and gives your Aussie a flat, non-slip place to lie down, but it does not hold the dog in place. Think of it as the floor of your setup, with a harness as the seat belt.

Where the hammock earns its place

During coat blow, an Aussie can leave a carpet of undercoat on a fabric seat in a single trip, and trail days bring mud, burrs and wet paws. A hammock catches all of it and goes in the washer. It also stops a dog from sliding into the footwell during braking, which helps nervous riders feel steadier. If you are tempted to shave your dog to cut the hair in the car, read our explanation of why Aussies are better left unshaved first; a washable hammock is the easier fix.

What to look for

Choose a hammock with slits for the seat-belt buckles, so you can clip a harness tether through it, and with straps that hold the front edge to the front headrests. A front panel that blocks the gap between the front seats takes away the view and the temptation to climb forward. Avoid hammocks that leave the dog standing on a soft, sagging surface; a firm, flat base is more comfortable for longer drives.

Step by step: switching your Aussie to a new car setup

Most dogs need practice before a new setup feels normal. Cornell notes that some dogs acclimate to car rides within a week, while others may take a few months, and recommends short, fun sessions of about 5 to 10 minutes. Move on only when your dog is relaxed at the current step; if it becomes anxious, you are going too fast.

  1. Introduce the gear at home. Crate: meals and chews inside. Harness: put it on, treat, take it off, building up to a few minutes of calm wear.
  2. Parked car, engine off. Invite your Aussie in, reward for going into the crate or onto the hammock, clip the tether, treat, unclip and finish.
  3. Add the sit-and-wait. Close the doors and sit in the driver's seat for a minute, with a chew or a lick mat for the dog.
  4. Engine on, no driving. Start the engine, wait 30 seconds, turn it off, reward and let the dog out.
  5. Drive to the end of the street. Short rides first: out of the driveway, around the block, to a nearby park. End at something fun, not only at the vet.
  6. Exercise before longer trips. Cornell recommends physical activity before the ride and frequent stops to stretch, relieve and drink. A sniffy walk works better than a frantic fetch session; our exercise needs by age guide helps you pick the right amount.
  7. Build a loading routine. Same door, same cue, same order every time: harness on or crate door open, dog in, clip or close, then you get in. Routines are what make herding dogs calm.

Quick pre-drive checklist

  • Crate strapped to the tie-down points, or tether clipped to the back of a car harness.
  • Tether too short to reach the front seats or the window.
  • Hammock front panel up, buckle slits lined up with the belt receivers.
  • Windows high enough that the head stays inside.
  • Water, a bowl, a leash, waste bags and a towel within reach of the driver's door.
  • A plan for stops; Cornell is clear that a dog should never be left unattended in the car, especially in warm or cold weather.

Common mistakes Aussie owners make with car setups

Buying the gear before teaching it. A new crate loaded on the morning of a road trip is a stranger in a moving box. Spend a week at home and in the parked car first.

Choosing the biggest crate that fits the cargo area. Extra room lets the dog slide around when you brake or corner. Size it to the dog, using the stand, turn and lie down rule.

Clipping a tether to a collar. Any sudden stop puts all the force on the neck. Always attach to the back of a car harness.

Treating the hammock as a restraint. It keeps the seats clean and the dog steady, but it does not stop a determined Aussie from climbing into the front.

Letting the dog ride in a pickup bed. Cornell says dogs should never ride loose in an open truck bed, even at low speed, because they can fall, jump or be thrown out.

Only driving to the vet. If every trip ends with a needle, the car becomes the warning sign. Mix in parks, trails and training class. Teenage Aussies in particular can suddenly object to routines they accepted as puppies; our teenage phase timeline explains why, and why patience pays off.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way for an Australian Shepherd to ride in the car?

For most Aussies with an SUV, wagon or minivan, a properly sized travel crate secured in the cargo area is the most contained option. In a sedan, a fitted car harness on a short tether behind the passenger seat works well, ideally with a hammock underneath for comfort and clean seats.

What size crate does an Australian Shepherd need for the car?

Measure your dog instead of buying by breed. The dog should be able to stand, turn around and lie down comfortably. Add a few inches to the height of the head while sitting and to the length from nose to tail base, then compare that with the maker's chart and with your cargo opening.

Can I use a back-seat hammock without a harness?

You can, but it gives no restraint. The hammock stops sliding into the footwell and protects the seats, while a car harness clipped through the buckle slits is what keeps your Aussie in place.

Is a regular walking harness good enough for the car?

No. Walking harnesses are made for leash walks, not for holding a dog in a seat. Use a dedicated car harness, look for independent crash testing, and attach the tether to the back of the harness, never to a collar.

How long does it take an Aussie to get used to a new car setup?

It varies. Cornell notes that some dogs settle into car travel within a week and others take a few months. Short, positive sessions of 5 to 10 minutes and a parked-car stage before any driving make the biggest difference.

My Aussie climbs into the front seat. Is it too late to change?

No. Adult dogs learn new car routines all the time. Start over as if the dog were new to the car: gear at home, parked car, engine on, then short drives, and reward lying down in the new spot every step of the way.

The bottom line: pick the setup your car and your Aussie can live with

There is no single perfect Australian Shepherd car setup, but there is a clear order of priorities: keep the dog contained or tethered, keep it out of the front of the car, and make the ride boring in the best possible way. A strapped-down crate suits cargo-space cars and busy dogs, a car harness on a short tether suits sedans and calm riders, and a hammock makes either option cleaner and steadier. If your current routine is a loose dog and a hand reaching back at every light, today is a perfectly good day to start again; run the planner, pick one setup and give your Aussie a week of short, happy practice.
