Australian Shepherd Teenage Phase: What Changes Between 6 and 24 Months and When They Finally Settle

Why your easy puppy turns into a selectively deaf teenager, what owners and researchers say about timing, and how to come out the other side with a dog people admire.

Home›Blog›Teenage phase

Published: 2026-10-20 · Australian Shepherd Club

The Australian Shepherd teenage phase usually starts somewhere between 6 and 9 months, when the puppy who aced every class suddenly acts as if "sit" is a foreign word. It is the stage where many owners quietly wonder whether they picked the wrong breed, and it is also the stage that decides which Aussies grow into the calm, admired dog everyone at the trailhead asks about. This guide lays out what actually changes month by month, what the research and hundreds of owner reports say about timing, and a training plan that gets you through it with your relationship intact.

Quick answer: Expect the hardest stretch between roughly 7 and 12 months, with selective deafness, jumping, mouthing and big reactions to other dogs. Most owners see the first real improvement somewhere between 12 and 18 months. Full settling takes longer: in one of the busiest owner threads on the breed, the middle answer for "when did yours calm down?" was about 3 years, and a sizeable minority said "never, but they learn an off switch." The off switch is trainable; the calendar is not.

Is the dog teenage phase real or just an excuse?

It is real, and it is measurable. A 2020 study of trainee guide dogs found that obedience to the main caregiver dropped at 8 months and recovered by 12 months, while obedience to a stranger did not drop at all.

The study, "Teenage dogs?" by Asher and colleagues in Biology Letters, tested 93 young Labradors, golden retrievers and crosses on a well-known "sit" at 5 and 8 months, and collected caregiver questionnaires on 285 dogs at 5, 8 and 12 months. At 8 months, the odds of a dog repeatedly ignoring "sit" from its own caregiver were about twice as high (odds ratio 2.14) as at 5 months. When a stranger gave the same cue, responses actually improved. Caregivers also rated their dogs less trainable at 8 months than at 5 or 12 months, and separation-related behavior scores were 36% higher at 8 months than at either end.

Why your Aussie ignores you and listens to the trainer

That carer-only drop is the most useful finding for owners. It means your teen is not "forgetting" training and the trainer is not magic. The dog is testing the relationship it cares about most. The researchers also found the dip was larger in dogs whose behavior suggested a less secure attachment to their carer. In plain terms: the more stable and predictable you are, the shorter and milder the rebellion tends to be.

What the study does not tell you about Aussies

No Australian Shepherds were in the sample, and the authors note that age windows need adjusting for different breeds. Treat 8 months as a reasonable center point, not a fixed date. Aussie owners commonly describe the trouble starting anywhere from about 6 to 11 months.

What changes between 6 and 24 months?

Four things change in sequence: reliability drops first, then impulse control is tested by bigger reactions, then guarding and alert barking appear, and finally the dog starts choosing calm on its own. The timing overlaps, but the order is remarkably consistent in owner reports.

6 to 9 months: the slide

Cues that worked in the kitchen fall apart on the sidewalk. Owners in an r/AustralianShepherd thread called "Finding adolescence hard" describe 8- to 10-month-old Aussies that jump on strangers, mouth hands without any aggression, counter-surf and "lose all interest in treats" once another dog appears. Several owners of 8- and 9-month-olds in that thread said they had a trainer and were doing everything right, and still saw this. That is the point: it is a stage, not a verdict on you.

9 to 15 months: the loud part

Leash excitement toward dogs and people peaks here for many Aussies. One owner described a 15-month-old who "was an angel baby until around 11 months" before the defiant streak appeared, which is a useful reminder that late bloomers exist. Another said the teen stage was "no cuddles," while the adult is "must cuddle."

12 to 18 months: the first switch

This is where the most hopeful reports cluster. One owner wrote that "between a year / year and a half, something just switched" and the biting, jumping and rough play stopped. A farm-working Aussie owner said that at 15 months their dog "started to get it" and fell into the daily routine. In the guide dog study, caregiver-rated trainability was back above its 5-month level by 12 months.

18 to 24 months and beyond: guarding grows, energy stays

Do not expect the energy to vanish at 2. What changes is control. In a thread asking whether Aussies got "more or less guardy after adolescence," most owners who answered either way said more: more barking at the door, more suspicion of visitors and delivery drivers, more watchfulness over their people. Plan for that rather than being surprised by it.

When do Australian Shepherds finally settle?

Most Aussies become noticeably easier between 12 and 18 months, but a genuinely settled adult usually arrives around 2 to 3 years. A smaller group keeps the teenage energy much longer, and many owners say their dog never fully "calms down" without a trained off switch.

We read through the replies to "When do they chill out?!" on r/AustralianShepherd, a thread with more than 260 upvotes and over 100 comments. Of the 18 replies that named an age when their Aussie calmed down, 4 said before age 2, 3 said around 2, 6 said around 3, 4 said between 4 and 5, and 1 said 12. The middle answer was 3 years. Roughly 15 more replies said some version of "they don't," and at least six said settling only came once they taught it on purpose, with a place cue, crate naps or a relaxation routine.

The American Animal Hospital Association's behavior management guidelines fit those owner reports well. They place social maturity in dogs at 12 to 36 months, describe it as a common time for behavior changes to appear, and note that while many dogs come out of it calmer and more attentive, there is no evidence that dogs simply "grow out" of real behavior problems. They also point out that most dogs given up to shelters for behavior reasons are 1 to 3 years old, right in this window.

What "settled" really means for this breed

A settled adult Aussie is not a couch potato. It is a dog that still sprints after a frisbee, but can lie on its mat during your video call, walk past a barking fence without exploding and take "that's enough" as an answer. That is the dog other owners admire, and it is built, not waited for.

Why do Aussie teenagers seem harder than other breeds?

Because the traits that make an adult Aussie impressive, such as speed, watchfulness and a strong drive to control movement, show up years before the judgment to use them well. A teenage Aussie has the engine of a working dog and the brakes of a puppy.

The herding wiring

Nipping at moving hands, circling children and staring down the cat are not random teenage quirks in this breed. They are herding behaviors without a job. If you live in a smaller space, our guide to managing herding instinct in an apartment has ideas for giving that drive a safe outlet.

Exercise is not the whole answer

Many replies in the threads above say "more exercise" fixed nothing until calm was trained too. Teenage Aussies can build stamina faster than you can tire them, and growing joints still set limits. The attachment piece

Aussies tend to bond hard to one person, and that is exactly the relationship the guide dog study found gets tested in adolescence. How to get through the Australian Shepherd teenage phase, step by step

This plan works because it reduces the number of times your teen gets to practice the wrong thing while you rebuild the right thing in easy conditions.

  1. Drop your expectations one level, on purpose. If "sit" fails outside, reward it inside the doorway for a week, then on the porch, then at the curb. Going back is not failing; it is how you stop rehearsing the ignored cue.
  2. Train a place or settle cue daily. Two or three sessions of 3 to 5 minutes, rewarding the dog for lying on a mat and relaxing its body. Add seconds, not minutes. This is the off switch owners in the threads above said made the difference.
  3. Schedule rest. Teen Aussies often get worse when overtired. Several owners recommended crate or pen naps after activity so the dog learns to switch off instead of looking for more input.
  4. Swap some walking for sniffing. Scent games and slow sniff walks tire the brain without winding the body up. Nosework came up repeatedly as the activity that helped most.
  5. Control greetings. Jumping is an impulse control problem. Ask for a sit before the door opens, before the food bowl goes down and before a visitor says hello. If the dog stands up, the good thing pauses.
  6. Give the guarding instinct a script. Teach a "thank you, that's enough" cue for door barking and practice visitor arrivals with a friend: the dog goes to its place, the visitor tosses a treat on the floor, nobody makes a fuss.
  7. Use distance with other dogs. Work at a distance where your Aussie can notice a dog and still take food. Reward the look and the turn back to you. Close the distance only when that is easy.
  8. Write down one win a day. Progress in this phase is uneven. A simple log stops one bad Tuesday from erasing three good weeks.

Teen-phase weekly checklist

  • Daily settle practice done at least once
  • Rest periods scheduled after the busiest part of the day
  • At least two sniff-heavy outings this week
  • Greeting rules used by everyone in the house
  • One new public place visited at a comfortable distance
  • No off-leash freedom where recall has not been proven

Common mistakes owners make in the teenage phase

Waiting for age to fix it. The AAHA guidelines are blunt that dogs do not simply grow out of behavior problems. Normal teenage silliness fades; practiced habits stay.

Adding exercise to solve impulse control. A fitter teen is not a calmer teen. Pair activity with trained rest.

Giving too much freedom too soon. Off-leash time at 10 months often turns a reliable puppy recall into an optional one. Use a long line until recall works with distractions.

Taking the carer-only disobedience personally. The research suggests the test is aimed at you because you matter most. Stay consistent and calm rather than louder.

Punishing alert barking. Guarding grows with maturity in many Aussies. Teach what to do instead, such as going to a mat, rather than only telling the dog to stop.

Comparing your dog with a different line or size. Working-line, show-line and miniature Aussies can mature on different schedules. If you are unsure what to expect from your dog's size, our mini vs standard Aussie size guide explains the overlap.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do Australian Shepherds hit the teenage phase?

Most owners report it starting between 6 and 9 months, with the hardest stretch around 7 to 12 months. In guide dog research, obedience to the main carer dipped at 8 months, though Aussies were not in that sample, so expect some variation.

How long does the Australian Shepherd teenage phase last?

The roughest part usually eases between 12 and 18 months. Social maturity in dogs spans roughly 12 to 36 months, so expect continued changes, especially more guarding, until about age 2 or 3.

Why does my teenage Aussie listen to other people but not me?

That pattern was documented in the 2020 "Teenage dogs?" study: adolescent dogs ignored their caregiver more often while still responding to a stranger. It reflects a test of the closest relationship, and consistent, calm handling shortens it.

Do Australian Shepherds ever calm down?

Most do become calmer, with owners most often naming about 3 years. Many owners say the energy never disappears, but a dog taught to settle on cue can switch off at home while still loving hard exercise.

Do Aussies get more protective after adolescence?

Many do. In an owner thread on exactly this question, most people who answered said their Aussie became more guarding after adolescence, with more barking at doors and suspicion of visitors. Teaching a visitor routine early helps.

Should I start obedience classes again during the teenage phase?

Yes, a class aimed at adolescent dogs can help, because it gives you structured practice around distractions. Choose a trainer who uses reward-based methods and lets you lower the difficulty when your teen is struggling.

The bottom line: the dog you are building

The Australian Shepherd teenage phase is real, it peaks in the second half of the first year, and it usually eases between 12 and 18 months, with full maturity closer to 2 or 3. You cannot speed up the calendar, but you can decide what your Aussie practices along the way. Keep criteria low, train the off switch daily and protect the relationship your teen is testing, and you end up with the Aussie people stop to compliment.
